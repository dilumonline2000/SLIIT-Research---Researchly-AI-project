1 · Overview

Module 4 is the analytics and foresight layer of the platform — it turns the corpus and activity generated by the other three modules into forward-looking, decision-supporting numbers. It covers:

  1. Trend forecasting — per-topic publication-count forecasts with confidence intervals, emerging-topic detection, and "where should I focus" recommendations.
  2. Quality scoring — four-dimension quality score (originality, citation impact, methodology, clarity) for any proposal/paper text.
  3. Success prediction — probability that a paper/proposal will be "successful" (publishable, methodologically sound), with actionable recommendations.
  4. Concept mind maps — department/topic-level concept graphs for exploratory visualisation.
  5. Cross-module dashboard — a single KPI snapshot aggregating data written by all four modules.

All five live in one FastAPI microservice (services/module4-analytics, port 8004), consumed by pages under apps/web/src/app/(dashboard)/analytics/ (trends, quality-scores, predictions, mind-maps, dashboards).

A recurring pattern in this module: every trained model (ARIMA, the two XGBoost regressor/classifier sets) is blended with a hand-calibrated heuristic rather than trusted alone at inference time. The code comments are explicit about why: the training heuristics the models learned from are narrow and can be unfair to legitimate technical/CS papers (e.g. penalising normal academic vocabulary length), so a calibrated heuristic acts as a fairness/safety net layered on top of the statistically-fit model rather than replacing it outright.

3 · Feature A — Trend Forecasting

Per-topic ARIMA with 95% confidence intervals

What it does

GET /trends returns, per research topic, a historical year-by-year publication count and a forecast for the next N years (with lower/upper confidence bounds), a trend-direction label (rising/declining/stable), a projected growth percentage, a plain-English interpretation, and the model's in-sample accuracy (RMSE/MAE). /trends/compare ranks multiple topics by growth; /trends/insights surfaces "emerging" topics and auto-generated research-area recommendations.

Why ARIMA per topic

Publication-count-over-time is a classic univariate time series, and ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) is well suited to short, noisy annual count series like this — it natively models trend (via the differencing/integration term) and produces principled confidence intervals from the fitted model's own residual distribution, which a naive linear-regression forecast would not give for free. A separate ARIMA model is fit per topic (rather than one global model) because different research areas have genuinely different growth dynamics.

How it is implemented (app/services/trend_forecaster.py)

  1. Loads models/trained_trend_forecaster/trend_models.pkl — a dict of fitted statsmodels ARIMA results objects, one per topic, each carrying its historical years/counts series.
  2. Forecast + CI (_forecast_with_ci) — calls the fitted model's get_forecast(steps=horizon), extracting the predicted mean and the 95% (alpha=0.05) prediction interval directly from statsmodels. Negative forecasts are clamped to 0 (a paper count can't be negative), and the upper bound is capped at 3× the historical maximum to guard against pathologically wide bands when residual variance is large. If the model object lacks get_forecast(), a heuristic fallback uses ±1.96·σ from the residual standard deviation instead.
  3. Trend direction (_trend_direction) — compares the mean of the last 3 years against the mean of everything before: >1.2× earlier → "rising", <0.8× → "declining", else "stable".
  4. Growth % — (forecast_end − latest_historical) / latest_historical × 100.
  5. Accuracy (_accuracy) — RMSE and MAE computed against the model's own in-sample fittedvalues (aligned to account for ARIMA's differencing dropping the first d observations), normalised into an nrmse (RMSE / mean) for a scale-free 0..1 quality indicator.

Emerging-topic detection

recent_slope = least_squares_slope(counts[last 4 years]) long_slope = least_squares_slope(counts[all years]) emergence = recent_slope − long_slope growth_ratio = latest_count / mean(counts[:-3]) score = 0.5 · emergence + 0.5 · growth_ratio (kept only if recent_slope > 0 and latest_count ≥ 2 — filters out noise from thin/declining topics)

Recommendation scoring

growth_norm = clamp(growth_pct / 100, −1.0, 2.0) saturation = latest_count(topic) / max_count(across all topics) score = growth_norm − 0.4 · saturation (rewards high projected growth, penalises topics that are already heavily published in — "low competition")

4 · Feature B — Quality Scoring

XGBoost regressors + calibrated heuristic blend

What it does

POST /quality/quality-score takes a title + abstract (or a stored proposal_id) and returns four 0–1 dimension scores — originality, citation_impact, methodology, clarity — plus an overall weighted score, the paper's predicted topic, and targeted recommendations for improvement.

Why a model + heuristic blend, and why the blend ratio varies per dimension

The trained XGBoost regressors were fit on a training heuristic that turned out to be unevenly reliable across the four dimensions — the code's own comments explain that methodology and originality are the most biased in the model (it over-relies on narrow keyword/length signals), so the heuristic is given almost full authority there, while citation_impact and clarity get more weight from the trained model since those signals generalise better. This produces a fairer score for legitimate technical/CS papers than either the raw model or the raw heuristic alone.

How it is implemented (app/services/quality_predictor.py)

  1. Feature extraction (extract_features) — word/sentence counts, average word/sentence length, a count of methodology keywords (25-term list, e.g. "experiment", "regression", "deep learning"), author count, and a citation-signal count (regex hits for [1]-style brackets, (Author, Year) parenthetical citations, and "et al." mentions in the abstract).
  2. Calibrated heuristic (_compute_quality_scores) — deliberately designed to separate genuine research writing from generic documents (forms, rubrics, reports):
    research_sig = min(1, count_of_matched_research_phrases / 4) e.g. "we propose", "this paper", "our approach" strong_method = count of 45 strict research-specific methodology terms (not generic words like "method") originality = min(1, 0.30·min(1, abstract_len/1200) + 0.70·research_sig) citation_impact = min(1, citation_base + (0.25 if multiple authors else 0)) citation_base: 0.70·min(1,signals/3) if signals>0, else 0.30 if long research text, 0.15 if some research signal, else 0.05 methodology = min(1, strong_method_count / 4) clarity = clamp(1 − max(0,(avg_word_len−7.5)/4) − max(0,(avg_sent_len−30)/30), 0.30, 1.0) overall = 0.30·originality + 0.25·citation_impact + 0.25·methodology + 0.20·clarity
  3. Model blend — if the trained bundle is available, each dimension's XGBoost prediction is blended with its heuristic counterpart at a dimension-specific ratio:
    DimensionModel weightHeuristic weightRationale (from code comments)
    methodology0.100.90Heuristic uses strict research-specific terms — near-fully authoritative
    originality0.150.85Heuristic uses research-phrase detection, not just length — strongly authoritative
    citation_impact0.200.80Model has some signal but heuristic still dominates
    clarity0.400.60Model is comparatively more reliable here
    overall is then recomputed from the blended dimension scores using the same 0.30/0.25/0.25/0.20 weights shown above, rather than blending the model's own overall output directly.

A companion function, is_research_document(), checks for the presence of standard academic sections (abstract/introduction/methodology/results/conclusion/references) before scoring, so a non-research document (e.g. an accidentally-uploaded form) can be flagged rather than silently scored as a weak paper.

5 · Feature C — Success Prediction

XGBoost classifier + heuristic safety net

What it does

POST /predict/predict (and /predict/upload for PDF/TXT files) returns a 0–1 success_probability, a binary successful / needs_improvement label, a confidence score, a low/medium/high risk level, and targeted improvement recommendations.

Why the same features feed a classifier here, not a regressor

This reuses the exact same extract_features() function as quality scoring (Section 4) but the trained artifact here is a binary XGBoost classifier — success is framed as a pass/fail outcome (publishable vs. not) rather than a continuous quality dimension, and predict_proba gives the probability of the positive class directly.

How it is implemented (app/services/success_predictor.py)

heuristic = _heuristic_score(features) — same structural formula as quality's overall score (0.30/0.25/0.25/0.20 weights) but with thresholds recalibrated specifically to avoid penalising technical/security papers: avg_word_length penalty starts at 7.5 (not 6.5), methodology needs only 4 keyword hits (not 6), citation_impact gives 0.25 partial credit even with zero abstract-citations model_proba = XGBoost.predict_proba(features)[1] (if trained bundle available) success_probability = 0.40 · model_proba + 0.60 · heuristic (heuristic dominates — code comment: "prevents extreme 0%/100% outputs for legitimate technical papers")

The final label thresholds at 0.5 (successful / needs_improvement); risk level is ≥0.7 low, ≥0.4 medium, else high. Recommendations are generated directly from which raw feature fell short (e.g. "Strengthen methodology section" if fewer than 3 methodology keywords were found, "Include citations…" if fewer than 2 citation signals). For PDF uploads, _extract_pdf_text uses PyMuPDF to pull text, then a regex-based heuristic (_META_RE) skips article-ID/DOI/copyright header lines to find the real title, and searches for an explicit "Abstract" marker before falling back to the first ~3500 characters of body text (skipping the first 500, likely header/author block) if no abstract section is found or it's too short.

6 · Feature D — Concept Mind Maps

KeyBERT keyword extraction + 2-layer GCN expansion

What it does

POST /mindmap/mindmap builds a department- or topic-scoped concept graph — nodes are concepts with an importance weight and a domain cluster, edges carry a relationship-strength — for the frontend's D3 force-directed visualisation.

Why KeyBERT + a graph neural network

This is a two-stage pipeline: KeyBERT first extracts a small set of seed keyphrases from the free-text topic (using MMR-diversified extraction so the seeds themselves aren't redundant), then a trained 2-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN, via torch_geometric) expands those seeds into a richer concept neighbourhood by propagating information across a pre-built concept graph — the GCN captures relationships between concepts that keyword extraction alone cannot (e.g. that "convolutional neural network" and "image classification" are related even without shared vocabulary).

How it is implemented

  1. Seed extraction (app/routers/mindmap.py) — if a topic is given, KeyBERT().extract_keywords(topic, keyphrase_ngram_range=(1,2), top_n=5, use_mmr=True, diversity=0.5) extracts up to 5 diverse 1–2-word seed concepts; on any import/runtime failure it falls back to simply splitting the topic string into its first 5 words.
  2. GCN expansion (app/models/mindmap_gnn.py::MindMapGNNModel) — the _GCNEncoder is a standard 2-layer GCNConv → ReLU → dropout(0.3) → GCNConv stack producing concept embeddings; weights are loaded from models/mindmap/model.pt with dimensions read from a bundled training_metadata.json. Seed concepts are matched to the model's known concepts.json vocabulary via case-insensitive substring matching; nearby concepts (by index proximity within ±3 — a proxy for graph adjacency baked in at training time) are added as related nodes, with edge weight decaying by 0.1 per index-step of distance (0.8 − |offset|·0.1). All seed nodes are additionally fully-connected to each other at a fixed weight of 0.9.
  3. Graceful degradation — if torch_geometric isn't installed or no seed concept matches the model's vocabulary, the router falls back to a trivial star graph (all seed keywords connected directly to the first seed) rather than returning an empty response.

7 · Feature E — Cross-Module Dashboard

Aggregation only, no ML

What it does

GET /analytics/dashboard returns a single KPI snapshot: total proposals, average quality score, top-5 trending topics, count of "at-risk" projects, and count of active supervisors.

How it is implemented (app/routers/dashboard.py)

This endpoint is a pure Supabase aggregation layer that pulls from tables written by every module — it is the one place in the codebase that visibly demonstrates cross-module integration:

KPISource tableComputation
Total proposalsresearch_proposalscount="exact" row count
Avg quality scorequality_scores (written by Feature B)mean of overall_score
Top trending topicsresearch_papers.keywords (written by Module 3's categorizer)top-5 most frequent keywords, up to 500 papers sampled
At-risk projectssuccess_predictions (written by Feature C)count where success_probability < 0.5
Active supervisorssupervisor_matches (written by Module 2)distinct supervisor_id where status = "active"

Every query is individually wrapped in its own try/except, so a failure in one table (e.g. a table not yet populated) degrades that one KPI to zero rather than failing the whole dashboard.

8 · Planned vs. Production Models

Spec designStatusWhat actually runs
ARIMA + Prophet ensemble, weighted by validation MAPE, target MAPE <22%, directional accuracy >75% Only ARIMA is wired into the live forecaster Per-topic ARIMA alone (Feature A) — accuracy is reported per-topic as RMSE/MAE/NRMSE rather than a single blended MAPE across a two-model ensemble
Success prediction — Random Forest (n=300, depth=8) + XGBoost (n=200, depth=6, lr=0.1) soft voting ensemble, target F1>0.75, ROC-AUC>0.80 Only XGBoost is present in the trained bundle XGBoost classifier blended 40/60 with a calibrated heuristic (Feature C) — the RF half of the planned soft-voting ensemble is replaced by the heuristic safety net instead
Why this matters for the report: both simplifications follow the same underlying engineering judgement seen across the whole platform — a single well-tuned model blended with an explicit, inspectable heuristic is easier to calibrate, debug and justify to a viva panel than a second black-box model stacked on top of the first, while still meeting the spirit of the spec's ensembling intent (combining two independent signal sources rather than trusting one score blindly).
